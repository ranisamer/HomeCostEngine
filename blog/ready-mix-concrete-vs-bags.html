Ready-Mix Concrete vs Bags: Which Makes Sense?

The cheapest material price is not always the cheapest pour. Compare volume, bag yield, mixing labor, placement time, truck access and supplier fees before choosing.

Reviewed September 21, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Bagged concrete is convenient because you can buy it in small quantities and mix it where you need it. Ready-mix concrete arrives as one larger batch, which can be valuable when a slab or footing needs continuous placement. The better option depends less on an arbitrary project-size cutoff and more on your actual volume and jobsite constraints.

Start with volume: Calculate cubic yards and approximate bag demand with the Concrete Calculator, then compare the total job—not only the sticker price per bag or per yard.

Convert cubic yards into bag demand correctly

One cubic yard equals 27 cubic feet. To estimate bags, divide the required cubic feet by the yield stated on the exact product bag. Do not assume every bag of the same weight has identical yield; mix design and manufacturer instructions can differ.

Example: a project needs 33.3 cubic feet before any allowance. If the selected product states 0.60 cubic feet of yield per bag, the theoretical requirement is 33.3 ÷ 0.60 ≈ 56 bags. That example illustrates the math only. Use the label on the product you are actually buying.

Why labor can outweigh the material difference

Every bag must be moved, opened, mixed with a controlled amount of water, discharged and followed by the next batch. On a larger pour, the crew can spend substantial time feeding a mixer instead of placing, screeding and finishing. If one end of a slab begins setting while the other end is still being mixed, finish consistency becomes harder to manage.

When comparing cost, assign a realistic value to mixer rental, labor hours, wheelbarrow handling, cleanup and the risk of delays. A bagged option that looks cheaper on material alone may not remain cheaper after those items are included.

Ready-mix pricing has more than a per-yard rate

A supplier quote may include or separately list delivery, minimum-load or short-load charges, waiting time, environmental fees, fuel-related charges, weekend or off-hour service, and specialty mix options. Ask for the full delivered price for the quantity and mix you need.

Also ask how the supplier rounds quantities and what happens if the truck cannot reach the planned discharge point. If a pump, concrete buggy or extra labor is required, include that before comparing the ready-mix total with bags.

Access can decide the answer

A truck being able to reach the property does not mean its chute can reach the forms. Measure the path from the safe truck position to the pour. Gates, soft ground, slopes, septic areas, overhead wires and landscaping can prevent close access.

Bagged concrete can be useful for a small backyard repair because the material can be carried in. For a larger slab far from the street, ready-mix may still be practical if the budget includes a pump or placement equipment.

Continuous pours deserve special attention

Slabs, walls and other placements can be sensitive to interruptions. If the project should be placed continuously, make sure the planned production rate can keep up with placement and finishing. Hand-mixing or a small mixer may be reasonable for limited work but can become the bottleneck as volume increases.

Three example decision patterns

Small repair or a few post holes

Bags are often practical because you can buy close to the needed quantity, transport them in a passenger vehicle or pickup, and mix each small placement as needed.

Medium patio with easy mixer access

Either method may work. Compare bag count and labor against the ready-mix delivered quote. The decision can swing based on short-load fees, crew size and whether the forms can be reached quickly.

Larger slab requiring coordinated finishing

Ready-mix can reduce mixing labor and support faster continuous placement, but only if truck access and discharge logistics are solved. A delayed or poorly planned truck pour can be as problematic as an underpowered bag-mixing setup.

Questions to ask a ready-mix supplier

  1. What is the delivered total for my quantity and specified mix?
  2. Is there a minimum-load or short-load charge?
  3. How is waiting time charged?
  4. What quantity increments can I order?
  5. How much chute reach is available from the truck?
  6. What site-access limitations should I plan for?
  7. What is the policy if I need an additional small quantity?

Questions to answer before choosing bags

  1. What yield does the exact bag label state?
  2. How many bags does the project require after the chosen allowance?
  3. Who will lift, mix and move them?
  4. Can the mixer maintain a consistent water ratio and production pace?
  5. Will the placement and finishing crew be waiting on the mixer?
  6. Where will bags be stored if weather changes?

Frequently asked questions

At what volume is ready-mix cheaper than bags?

There is no universal cutoff. Local bag prices, product yield, delivery fees, short-load charges, labor and access determine the break-even point. Compare the full delivered and labor-adjusted totals for your project.

Are bags stronger than ready-mix?

Strength depends on the specified product or mix and correct water, mixing, placement and curing—not simply whether the concrete arrived in a bag or truck.

Can I mix part of a slab with bags and finish with ready-mix?

That can create timing and consistency problems. For a slab intended as a continuous placement, plan the supply method before work begins and discuss construction joints or interruptions with a qualified professional when relevant.
