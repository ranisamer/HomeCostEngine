Questions to Answer Before Budgeting a Garage Conversion

First define what replaces the garage and what happens to everything it does today.

HomeCostEngine Editorial · October 30, 2026

A garage conversion starts with two linked decisions: the use you want to create and the functions you will displace. Asking for a price before either is defined can produce offers for very different projects. Prepare a short feasibility request before treating a room-package price as a complete budget.

1. What use are you actually asking to create?

Describe daytime use, sleeping, cooking, bathing, separate access and intended occupancy. Ask your actual authority and designer how that proposal is classified. Portland's conversion page distinguishes its habitable-space process from ADU permitting and discusses permit history. That is a local example of the questions to ask, not permission or a code standard for another city.

2. What property evidence is still missing?

List accessible drawings, prior permits and relevant professional reports with dates. Mark listing descriptions and owner assumptions separately. A garage that looks finished is not evidence that the intended use has been approved. Ask which documents the reviewer needs, rather than filling gaps with a neighbor's experience.

3. Where do parking and stored items go?

Write every function the garage currently supplies, then name its proposed replacement. Check practical access, capacity, actual permissions and recurring charges. Do not assume that storing items in another room costs nothing, or that a driveway automatically resolves every local parking question.

4. What area is the price divided by?

Original invented example: a 22 by 20 foot garage has a 440-square-foot gross footprint. A concept reserves 40 square feet for entry/service and 60 for storage, leaving 340 for the proposed room. A $44,880 package is $102 per gross square foot but $132 per proposed-room square foot.

The price stayed the same; only the denominator changed. Neither ratio establishes legal living area or a local market price. Ask bidders to identify the zones receiving work and the area definition behind any ratio.

5. Which garage-specific findings could change the concept?

Request qualified review of the existing building, former vehicle-door opening, floor, enclosure and service interfaces. Record past water entry as an observation, not a diagnosis. EPA's moisture guide emphasizes moisture control. Ask the relevant professional what must be resolved before enclosing affected areas; a decorative finish is not readiness evidence.

For relevant older housing, EPA's consumer renovation page addresses lead-safe work in pre-1978 homes. Ask qualified parties about actual applicability. This article gives no sampling, cleanup or construction procedure.

6. What sits outside the construction quote?

Request separate identification of assessment, design, applications, supporting work, displaced storage and other owner arrangements. Keep amounts already paid, committed and merely forecast distinct. Do not fund the project with assumed rental income, resale value or an unverified area increase.

7. What answer changes proceed to revise or pause?

Finish the free checklist by naming one unresolved issue, the party responsible and the evidence needed. A useful outcome might be 'clarify intended occupancy before design' or 'assess the garage floor before accepting a finish package.' A forecast date is not permission to occupy.

Once scope is defined, the free quote comparison tool helps organize offers. The Garage Conversion Decision Workbook adds four complete cases, seven worksheets, 35 fillable fields and two completed records in a 26-page PDF for £9.99 GBP. Its other cases cover displaced-storage cost, a budget gap after a new finding and parallel schedule dependencies.
