How Much Gravel Do I Need for a Driveway?

Estimate driveway gravel in cubic yards and tons from length, width, compacted depth, material density and order allowance.

Published September 8, 2026 • Reviewed October 10, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Quick answer: driveway gravel volume in cubic yards = length in feet × width in feet × layer depth in inches ÷ 324. For a 100 × 12-foot driveway with one 4-inch layer, the geometric volume is 14.81 cubic yards. Delivered tons require the supplier's conversion on the same loose or compacted basis. A chosen 10% allowance and illustrative density of 1.50 U.S. tons per cubic yard produce 24.44 tons only under those stated assumptions.

Use the driveway gravel quantity and cost calculator to run a rectangular, single-layer scenario now. Enter measured dimensions, the specified depth, a deliberate allowance and your quoted prices. The guide below explains how to handle wider entrances, separate layers and supplier conversions before placing an order.

Start with the work you are actually doing

A surface refresh adds material over an adequate existing structure. A rebuild may remove failed material and install separate base and surface layers. Filling wheel ruts is a local repair, not the same as placing a uniform layer over the full driveway. Agree on the work and finished levels before measuring; additional stone alone does not resolve a soft subgrade or a drainage problem.

Have the installer specify each layer's product and thickness for your soil, drainage, slope and vehicle loads. The 4-inch example on this page demonstrates quantity math; it is not a universal driveway specification.

Measure the footprint without counting overlaps

Sketch the main drive, parking pad, turnaround and entrance. Measure usable length and width in feet. For a taper, measure both end widths and multiply its length by the average width. A triangle has half the area of a rectangle with the same base and perpendicular height. Divide curves into short measured sections rather than treating the whole site as one large bounding rectangle.

For example, a 100 × 12-foot main drive has 1,200 square feet. A separate triangular flare with a 20-foot base and 12-foot perpendicular height adds 120 square feet, making 1,320 total. This addition is correct only if the triangle is outside the original rectangle. Draw shared boundaries and exclude planted islands. The square footage calculator helps check component areas before converting them to volume.

Worked example: 100 feet long, 12 feet wide, 4 inches deep

Area100 × 121,200 ft²
Geometric layer volume1,200 × 4 ÷ 32414.8148 yd³
Illustrative 10% allowance14.8148 × 1.1016.2963 yd³
Illustrative weight conversion16.2963 × 1.5024.4444 U.S. tons

Rounded display values are 14.81 yd³ before allowance, 16.30 yd³ after allowance and 24.44 tons. The divisor 324 comes from 12 inches per foot and 27 cubic feet per cubic yard. Keep the unrounded quantity through the calculation; round the final order according to the supplier's sales increment.

If the supplier confirms the same conversion basis and sells in half-ton increments, this illustrative 24.4444-ton quantity rounds upward to 24.5 tons. That does not establish the number of truckloads: ask about legal payload, access and the delivery charge for a partial final load. A truckload is not a standard unit of volume.

Compacted depth is not automatically delivered loose volume

The volume formula calculates the space occupied at the depth you enter. Stone delivered loose can occupy a different volume after placement and compaction. Ask the supplier or installer: “For this product, how many delivered tons are required per cubic yard of the specified compacted layer?” A loose bulk-density figure answers a different question unless a compatible conversion has been applied.

The calculator multiplies geometric volume by your selected allowance and its displayed planning density. It does not model a product-specific compaction curve. If your supplier provides a different tons-per-yard figure, multiply the confirmed volume manually by that figure. If it instead quotes delivered tons per compacted cubic yard, use that conversion directly and keep any additional loss allowance separate.

Ten percent is an example, not a compaction rule. Do not add another compaction factor when the supplier's quantity already includes it. Record what each adjustment covers: measurement uncertainty, placement loss or conversion from loose to compacted material. Moisture and aggregate grading can also affect weight and handling; a generic density cannot resolve them.

Calculate base and surface layers separately

Suppose an installer specifies a 6-inch base and a 2-inch surface course over the same 1,200-square-foot footprint. These hypothetical thicknesses illustrate the takeoff, not a design recommendation. The base is 1,200 × 6 ÷ 324 = 22.22 yd³; the surface is 1,200 × 2 ÷ 324 = 7.41 yd³, both geometric volumes before any conversion or allowance.

Send the supplier two lines with separate product names, thicknesses and conversion bases. Do not apply the surface product's density to the combined 29.63 yd³ if the base differs. Run the calculator once per layer, then combine the confirmed material quantities and prices. Avoid counting a shared delivery or grading charge twice.

The EPA-hosted Surface Gravel manual section distinguishes aggregate for a driving surface from material for other uses. Its road-maintenance guidance explains why product grading matters; it is background rather than a private-driveway design specification.

Allow for ruts and changes in depth explicitly

Measure known depressions as additional volume instead of hiding them in a percentage. If a 10 × 12-foot patch needs an extra 2 inches beyond the uniform layer, add 10 × 12 × 2 ÷ 324 = 0.74 yd³. Count only the extra thickness in that patch because the uniform layer already includes it. Confirm whether the repair needs removal or base correction before filling it.

Depth errors can outweigh small rounding differences. One additional inch over 1,200 square feet adds 3.70 yd³ before allowance. Check several locations and the intended finished level. For a surface refresh, record the new material being added rather than estimating the full existing structure again.

How much area does a yard or ton cover?

Geometrically, one cubic yard covers 324 ÷ depth in inches square feet: 162 ft² at 2 inches or 81 ft² at 4 inches. At the illustrative 1.50-ton-per-yard conversion, one ton corresponds to 0.6667 yd³ and about 54 ft² at 4 inches. Those figures exclude allowance and depend on using a compatible volume basis; they are not a promise of compacted coverage from a supplier's loose ton.

Turn the quantity into a useful supplier request

  1. Provide the footprint sketch and measured area for every section.
  2. List each aggregate product and its specified finished thickness separately.
  3. Ask whether quantities and density refer to loose or compacted material.
  4. Show known extra repair volume and any chosen allowance once.
  5. Confirm the sales unit, rounding increment, truck capacity and delivery access.
  6. Record placement, grading, compaction, drainage and disposal inclusions.

Use the complete installed gravel driveway budget method after quantities are confirmed. Material and freight do not automatically include excavation, fabric, culverts or installation. Put the supplier's takeoff beside contractor proposals in the contractor quote comparison tool so the cheapest material line is not mistaken for the cheapest complete project.

Save dimensions, product names, exclusions and unresolved questions in the free home-project planning workbook. Return to the gravel calculator when the supplier confirms the conversion or a contractor changes the layer depth, then update the worksheet rather than ordering from the first rough estimate.

Drainage, local rules and regional differences

The EPA-hosted Drainage manual section explains the role of water management in gravel-road performance. A quantity estimate cannot choose a safe slope, repair drainage or authorize a runoff outlet. Ask a qualified local installer about wet or weak ground and the appropriate structure before buying material.

Available quarry products, frost conditions, rainfall, grades and delivery access differ across the United States. Check local requirements before altering a road entrance, ditch, culvert or right-of-way. Confirm any underground utility locating needed before excavation. Drainage work, engineered design, unsuitable-soil removal and entrance improvements remain separate scope items unless expressly included.

Frequently asked questions

How much gravel does a 100 by 12-foot driveway need?

One 4-inch layer has a geometric volume of 14.81 cubic yards. A chosen 10% allowance gives 16.30 cubic yards. At an illustrative compatible conversion of 1.50 U.S. tons per cubic yard, that is 24.44 tons; confirm the supplier's loose or compacted basis.

Is a 10% allowance enough for compaction?

No universal percentage can answer that. Confirm the product-specific loose-to-compacted conversion and keep any separate loss allowance visible without counting compaction twice.

Can I combine base and surface gravel in one calculation?

Calculate each specified layer separately. Different products can have different conversions and prices, even when they cover the same footprint.

How do I estimate gravel for a wider driveway entrance?

Measure the added section as a rectangle, triangle or taper. Add only its non-overlapping area, then apply the specified layer depth.

How many truckloads should I order?

First confirm delivered tons or yards, then ask the supplier about legal payload, access and partial-load charges. Truckload capacity is not a standard quantity.
