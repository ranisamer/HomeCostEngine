How Much Extra Concrete Should I Order?

Start with measured volume, then add a project-specific allowance for ordinary field variation—not a guess large enough to hide incomplete measurements.

Reviewed September 18, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Ordering exactly the theoretical concrete volume leaves no room for a slightly deeper excavation, bowed forms, an irregular base or material left in placement equipment. Ordering too much wastes material and can create a disposal problem. The right allowance is therefore not one universal percentage: it is a documented adjustment based on measurement confidence, slab complexity and the supplier’s ordering rules.

For a well-measured rectangular residential slab, homeowners can compare a low, typical and high planning scenario before confirming the order with the concrete contractor or ready-mix producer. The allowance is a budgeting tool, not a substitute for a site check or engineered design.

Begin with the theoretical volume

For a rectangular slab, calculate cubic feet as length × width × thickness in feet, then divide by 27 to convert cubic feet to cubic yards. A four-inch slab uses a thickness of 4 ÷ 12, or 0.333 feet. Measure inside the forms rather than relying on a plan dimension that may not match the built work.

Use separate calculations for steps, piers, footings and thickened edges. Do not apply the slab thickness to the entire footprint if the design has multiple depths. The Concrete Calculator helps test several dimensions and allowances without changing the underlying formula.

Low, typical and high allowance scenarios

The percentages below are planning scenarios, not building-code requirements or guarantees. Select the scenario only after checking the forms and base:

  • Low allowance—about 3%: a simple shape, accurately set forms, a level and compacted base, verified thickness and direct placement with little handling.
  • Typical allowance—about 7%: an ordinary residential slab with minor base variation, several corners or a modest amount of wheelbarrow or pump handling.
  • High allowance—about 12%: irregular geometry, uncertain excavation, multiple depths, long placement travel or conditions that make the measured volume less certain.

A high percentage should trigger better measurement rather than automatic over-ordering. If the excavation is visibly uneven or the design includes structural thickening, calculate those volumes directly first and use the allowance only for normal variation.

Worked example: a 20-by-24-foot slab

Consider a 20 × 24 foot slab designed at 4 inches thick. Its theoretical volume is:

20 × 24 × (4 ÷ 12) ÷ 27 = 5.93 cubic yards.

The same measured slab produces these planning quantities before supplier rounding:

  • Low 3% scenario: 5.93 × 1.03 = 6.11 cubic yards.
  • Typical 7% scenario: 5.93 × 1.07 = 6.34 cubic yards.
  • High 12% scenario: 5.93 × 1.12 = 6.64 cubic yards.

If the local producer accepts half-yard increments, those calculated amounts may become 6.5, 6.5 and 7.0 yards respectively, subject to the producer’s minimum load and rounding policy. Ask whether the quoted quantity, delivery charge, short-load fee and environmental or returned-concrete fee are separate line items. HomeCostEngine does not assign a price here because local material, fuel and delivery conditions vary.

Add thickened edges without double counting

Suppose the same slab has a 12-inch-wide perimeter edge that is 8 inches total depth. The main slab calculation already includes the first 4 inches, so only the additional 4 inches should be added. The approximate perimeter is 88 feet. Additional volume is:

88 × 1 × (4 ÷ 12) ÷ 27 = 1.09 cubic yards.

The revised theoretical order is 5.93 + 1.09 = 7.02 cubic yards before any allowance. Calculating the full eight-inch edge and then adding it to the four-inch slab would count the overlapping four inches twice. For more multi-part examples, see How Many Cubic Yards Do I Need?

Where the extra concrete actually goes

Common field variation includes shallow depressions in the aggregate base, small differences between planned and built form dimensions, forms that move during placement, concrete retained in a pump line or wheelbarrow, and minor spillage. None of those allowances should cover an omitted footing, an unmeasured step or an unapproved change in slab thickness.

The National Ready Mixed Concrete Association’s CIP 8 on yield discrepancies distinguishes the volume of fresh concrete produced from the quantity that a project consumes. Apparent shortages can result from actual slab dimensions, deflection or irregular subgrade rather than a producer delivering less material. Its CIP 31 ordering guide also emphasizes communicating quantity, strength, aggregate, slump, air and delivery requirements—not quantity alone.

Scope inclusions and exclusions

A concrete quantity normally covers the fresh concrete placed inside the measured forms. It does not automatically include excavation, compacted stone, reinforcement, vapor barrier, forming lumber, pumping, finishing, curing materials, saw-cut joints, demolition, washout handling or disposal of returned concrete. These items belong in the project scope even though they are not part of the cubic-yard formula.

Bagged concrete is sold by dry bag yield rather than truck cubic yards. For a small landing, post base or repair, compare the manufacturer’s stated yield per bag with the required cubic feet. For a larger continuous slab, read Ready-Mix Concrete vs. Bags before deciding how material will reach the forms.

Regional, permit and safety checks

Climate, soil, frost depth, expansive soils, drainage and intended loads can change the required base, reinforcement, footing depth and slab design. A patio, driveway, garage slab and structural foundation are not interchangeable. Local building departments may require permits, setbacks, inspections or engineered details, especially for foundations, garages, retaining conditions and work near utilities.

Confirm underground utility locations before excavation and keep people clear of placement equipment. Concrete formwork and shoring are safety-critical; the federal OSHA concrete and masonry construction standard includes requirements for formwork, reinforcing steel and concrete placement in covered workplaces. Homeowners should use qualified local professionals for structural work and verify which rules apply to their project.

A pre-order checklist

  1. Measure the inside length and width of every formed section.
  2. Check slab depth at several locations and correct low or soft base areas.
  3. Calculate slabs, thickened edges, footings, steps and piers separately.
  4. Add the volumes, then choose a documented low, typical or high allowance.
  5. Ask the supplier about order increments, minimum loads, delivery access, spacing between trucks and returned-concrete policies.
  6. Confirm mix specifications, placement method, crew size, weather plan and permit inspections with the contractor.

For a common small-project comparison, the 12×12 slab quantity guide shows how thickness changes the order. Recheck every input on pour day; a percentage cannot rescue a calculation made from the wrong dimensions.

Run your own numbers: Enter each section separately in the Concrete Calculator, compare the three allowance scenarios, and confirm the final order with your contractor or ready-mix supplier.

Frequently asked questions

Is 10% extra concrete always enough?

No. Ten percent is a common rule of thumb, not a universal requirement. A precise simple slab may need less contingency, while an irregular or poorly measured excavation may need direct remeasurement rather than a larger percentage.

Should I round the concrete order up?

Usually the calculated amount must be reconciled with the producer’s accepted order increments and minimum-load policy. Confirm the rounding rule and fees directly; do not assume every supplier sells the same increments.

Does a waste allowance include thickened edges?

No. Calculate known thickened edges, footings, steps and piers as separate geometry. Apply a contingency only after all designed concrete volumes are included.

What happens if the truck has concrete left over?

The producer or contractor follows its return and washout procedure, and charges may apply. Never plan to dump concrete on the property without an approved location and safe handling plan.

Can I order the day of the pour?

Availability varies by market and season. Coordinate the mix, quantity, delivery interval, access and placement equipment in advance, particularly when the pour must be continuous.
