How Much Extra Concrete Should I Order?

Start with measured volume, then add a project-specific allowance for ordinary field variation, not a guess large enough to hide incomplete measurements.

Reviewed September 18, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Extra concrete should be based on project uncertainty, not a universal percentage. Start with accurate geometry, then identify the reasons your actual placement could require more material than the theoretical volume. The cubic-yard calculation guide helps establish the base volume before any extra quantity is added.

Reasons a concrete order may need an allowance

  • Subgrade is not perfectly level
  • Forms bow or dimensions vary
  • Thickened edges or footings were not included in the first calculation
  • Some material remains in the chute, pump or buggy
  • Spillage or placement losses occur
  • Supplier order increments require rounding

Measure thickened sections separately

A specific 20-by-20 slab worked example shows how thickness and edge volume change the order without counting the same concrete twice.

Edges, beams, footings and steps can add substantial volume. Calculate them as separate shapes and add them to the slab volume before applying any allowance.

How much extra concrete for uneven subgrade and a thickened edge?

First measure the actual shapes rather than hiding missing concrete inside a generic waste percentage. Suppose a hypothetical 12-by-16-foot patio has a uniform 4-inch slab: 12 × 16 × (4 ÷ 12) = 64 cubic feet, or 64 ÷ 27 = 2.37 cubic yards. If one 16-foot edge has an additional 4 inches of depth over a 1-foot-wide strip, count only that extra depth: 16 × 1 × (4 ÷ 12) = 5.33 cubic feet, or 0.20 cubic yard. The geometric total is about 2.57 cubic yards before allowance.

If measured low spots across 48 square feet average an additional half inch of concrete, the extra volume is 48 × (0.5 ÷ 12) = 2 cubic feet, or about 0.074 cubic yard. The revised measured total is about 2.64 cubic yards. An illustrative 5% placement allowance then produces 2.64 × 1.05 = 2.77 cubic yards before supplier rounding. Do not apply an allowance to compensate for a low spot you already measured and added. Do not multiply the edge by its full depth if the base slab volume already covers the first 4 inches.

This calculation is an order-planning example, not a prescribed patio design. A professional should confirm subgrade, thickness, reinforcement, drainage and any footing or permit requirements for the actual site. Verify the ready-mix supplier’s delivery increment and short-load terms before the final order.

Supplier increments can matter more than the percentage

If the supplier only accepts certain order increments or applies short-load minimums, the practical order may be driven by those rules. Ask about minimums, increments and the policy for adding material on pour day.

Running short is not the same as ordering too much

A shortage can interrupt placement and create construction-joint or finishing problems. Too much concrete can create disposal and washout issues. The right order balances measurement confidence with the cost and risk of shortage.

Better ordering workflow

  1. Measure every slab, edge, footing and step.
  2. Calculate theoretical volume.
  3. Check form dimensions and subgrade.
  4. Add only a justified allowance.
  5. Confirm supplier minimums and increments.
  6. Review placement access and pump/chute losses.
