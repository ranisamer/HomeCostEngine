Concrete Driveway Cost Per Square Foot: 2026 Planning Guide

Use cost per square foot to compare driveway proposals only after area, removal, base, slab design, reinforcement, access and finish are normalized.

Reviewed October 2, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Quick answer: an installed concrete-driveway price per square foot is the contractor’s total for a defined scope divided by the measured driveway area. It is useful for comparing bids, but it is not a stand-alone market price. Two proposals can use the same square-foot figure while including different demolition, base, slab thickness, reinforcement, drainage, finish, curing and permit work.

Start by measuring the paved footprint and testing the concrete volume with the Concrete Calculator. Then compare written scopes line by line. The calculation should help you find omissions; it should not replace a local site visit or structural decision.

The formula—and its most important limitation

Installed cost per square foot = comparable project total ÷ measured driveway area

The numerator must represent the same work in every quote. If Bid A includes removal and disposal while Bid B excludes them, dividing each sticker price by area creates a misleading comparison. First add confirmed exclusions and allowances, or keep those line items separate until every bidder has priced the same scope.

Measure only the surfaces actually being replaced. A 20-by-36-foot rectangle is 720 square feet. Add turnouts, parking pads or aprons as separate rectangles or triangles, and subtract areas that remain untouched. Do not use the home’s floor area as a proxy for driveway area.

Worked quantity example: 18 × 40 feet

Consider a hypothetical rectangular driveway measuring 18 feet by 40 feet. Its surface area is:

18 × 40 = 720 square feet

At a uniform 4-inch thickness, concrete depth is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 foot. The theoretical volume is 720 × 0.333 ÷ 27 = about 8.89 cubic yards. At 5 inches, volume becomes 720 × (5 ÷ 12) ÷ 27 = about 11.11 cubic yards. The extra inch increases theoretical volume by 25% before any ordering allowance.

A 7% planning allowance would bring those quantities to about 9.51 and 11.89 cubic yards respectively, before supplier rounding. This is quantity math, not a design recommendation. Read the driveway thickness planning guide and follow the local design or contractor specification rather than choosing thickness from price alone.

Use low, typical and high-complexity scenarios

Instead of applying one national price, classify the project’s scope and request local quotes for the matching condition:

  • Lower-complexity scope: clear truck access, simple rectangular geometry, suitable existing subgrade, little or no removal, standard broom finish and no major drainage correction.
  • Typical full replacement: demolition and disposal, excavation, a documented aggregate base, reinforcement, formed edges, joints, curing, cleanup and ordinary access constraints.
  • Higher-complexity scope: poor or expansive soil, steep grade, drainage work, retaining conditions, thickened sections, difficult access, pumping, decorative finish, utility conflicts or extensive restoration.

These labels describe scope, not price promises. Ask every contractor which scenario the bid assumes and which conditions can trigger a change order.

Base preparation can explain a large price difference

A low proposal may assume the existing material can remain, while another includes excavation and compacted aggregate. Ask bidders to state the compacted base depth—not only the loose delivery depth—and how unsuitable soil is handled. Uniform support matters; the American Concrete Pavement Association’s technical bulletin emphasizes the role of subgrade and subbase variables in producing stable, uniform pavement support.

Drainage should also be explicit. Surface water needs a planned path away from structures and neighboring property. Where grading cannot solve the issue, a drain, curb, channel or other work may be separate. Local conditions such as frost-susceptible soil, expansive clay and high groundwater can change both the design and the excavation scope.

Do not compare reinforcement by name alone

“Reinforced” is incomplete without the material, size, spacing, laps, support and intended position. Reinforcement may help control crack widths or serve other design purposes, but it does not make subgrade preparation, joints or curing optional. Ask who is responsible for the design and whether the proposal follows local requirements and the intended vehicle loads.

Heavy trucks, recreational vehicles, delivery traffic or frequent turning near slab edges can require a different design from passenger-car use. Do not assume a thicker slab or more steel is automatically correct; obtain a site-specific recommendation when loads or soil conditions are unusual.

Jointing and curing belong in the price

Concrete shrinks as it dries, so the quote should describe the planned panel layout and joint method. The National Ready Mixed Concrete Association’s slab-on-grade joint guidance discusses spacing, panel shape, joint depth and cutting timing. Ask the installer to show the proposed joint pattern before placement, especially around curves, inside corners and utility penetrations.

Curing is a process, not merely waiting for the slab to look dry. The American Concrete Institute’s curing guide covers methods for pavements and other slabs on ground. Have the proposal state who supplies and applies curing materials, how the surface will be protected, and when normal foot and vehicle traffic may return under the contractor’s instructions.

Ready-mix delivery and site access

The material quote may not include short-load charges, waiting time, off-hour delivery, pumping, buggies or extra labor when the truck cannot reach the forms. Confirm safe truck position, chute reach, overhead wires, gates, slopes, septic areas and washout location before comparing totals.

The ready-mix versus bagged concrete guide explains why continuous placement and labor capacity matter. For a driveway-sized pour, the planning question is usually not only material cost; it is whether the crew and delivery method can place, screed, finish and protect the slab as designed.

How to normalize two real contractor quotes

  1. Fix the area. Give every bidder the same measured sketch and ask them to verify it onsite.
  2. Fix the design assumptions. Compare the same slab thickness, edge details, base, reinforcement and finish—or clearly record why they differ.
  3. List included quantities. Record square feet, demolition volume, base depth, concrete volume and reinforcement quantity where available.
  4. Price exclusions. Obtain written allowances or separate local prices for disposal, pumping, drainage, permits, sealing and restoration.
  5. Calculate an adjusted total. Add required excluded scope to each bid without double-counting an item already included.
  6. Divide by the same area. Compare adjusted total ÷ verified square feet, then review warranty, schedule and change-order terms separately.

The Contractor Quote Comparison tool helps organize the totals, allowances and exclusions. A lower adjusted price can still be a poor choice if the scope remains ambiguous or the payment schedule, license, insurance or warranty cannot be verified.

What is usually included—and commonly excluded

A complete replacement proposal may include layout, demolition, hauling, excavation, base preparation, forms, concrete, reinforcement, placement, finishing, joints, curing, basic cleanup and final grading. Common exclusions include hidden soil correction, utility relocation, drainage structures, retaining work, permits, engineering, decorative upgrades, sealing, landscaping, irrigation repair, gate work and adjacent sidewalk or curb work.

Do not assume a public apron, sidewalk crossing or curb cut belongs to the same private-driveway scope. Work in the public right-of-way can require separate permits, inspections, bonding or an approved contractor. Homeowners-association approval may be separate from municipal approval.

Regional and permit caveats

Driveway requirements vary by city, county, climate and site. Frost depth, expansive soil, stormwater rules, maximum slope, fire access, right-of-way construction, tree protection and impervious-surface limits can change the work. Contact the local building, public works or transportation department before signing, and ask whether the contractor or owner obtains the permit and schedules inspections.

Call the local utility-notification service before excavation as required. Fresh concrete can irritate or burn skin, and demolition creates dust and equipment hazards; keep children and visitors away and leave placement and removal work to properly equipped crews.

Frequently asked questions

What should concrete driveway cost per square foot include?

It should be tied to a written scope. Confirm whether the total includes removal, disposal, excavation, compacted base, slab design, reinforcement, forms, delivery, placement, finish, joints, curing, permits and cleanup.

Does a thicker driveway always cost more?

Greater thickness increases concrete volume and may affect excavation, forms and reinforcement. In the 720-square-foot example, moving from four to five inches raises theoretical concrete volume from about 8.89 to 11.11 cubic yards, a 25% increase.

Should I choose the lowest square-foot quote?

Only after adjusting the bids to the same measured area and equivalent scope. Review exclusions, allowances, design assumptions, license and insurance, schedule, payment terms, change-order rules and warranty before deciding.

Is sealing included in a driveway quote?

Not necessarily. Ask whether a sealer is specified, which product is proposed, when it will be applied and whether surface preparation and return visits are included.

Do I need a driveway permit?

Requirements vary. Private slab work, curb cuts, sidewalks, drainage and public-right-of-way work may be treated differently. Confirm with the local authority and homeowners association before work begins.
