How Much Mulch Do I Need?

Mulch quantity is a volume problem: area tells you how large the bed is, and depth tells you how much material it needs.

Reviewed September 30, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Quick answer: measure the square feet of bed you will actually cover, choose the additional mulch depth in inches, and divide area × depth by 324 to get cubic yards. For a 500-square-foot bare bed at 3 inches, that is 500 × 3 ÷ 324 = 4.63 cubic yards before a modest allowance for measurement error. If the bed already has usable mulch, calculate only the depth needed to reach the target layer.

The formula behind a mulch order

One cubic yard contains 27 cubic feet. An inch is one twelfth of a foot, so cubic yards = square feet × (inches ÷ 12) ÷ 27, or square feet × inches ÷ 324. This calculates loose geometric volume at the planned depth, not the delivered price or an exact number of truckloads. The Mulch Calculator performs this conversion and accepts a local material price per cubic yard.

Measure each bed before combining areas. A rectangle is length × width. For a roughly triangular section, use base × height ÷ 2; for a circular bed, use π × radius² and subtract the space you will leave bare around stems. Long curved borders can be divided into short rectangles. A tape measure and a simple sketch are more reliable than using the entire lot size or the home's floor area.

Worked example: two beds with a path

Suppose a homeowner has a 20 × 10-foot bed and a 12 × 8-foot bed. Their rectangular areas are 200 and 96 square feet. A 4 × 4-foot stepping-stone area in the first bed will not be mulched, so subtract 16 square feet. The actual area is 200 + 96 − 16 = 280 square feet.

At 2.5 inches of new mulch, the calculated volume is 280 × 2.5 ÷ 324 = 2.16 cubic yards. If the homeowner wants a 10% planning allowance for small measurement errors and uneven spreading, 2.16 × 1.10 = 2.38 cubic yards. A supplier selling in half-yard increments could quote 2.5 yards; the allowance and rounding are planning choices, not universal rules. Ask the supplier how loads are measured and what increments are available.

For 2-cubic-foot bags, 2.38 cubic yards is 2.38 × 27 = about 64.3 cubic feet, or 32.2 bags. Round up to 33 bags. If buying the rounded 2.5-yard bulk equivalent instead, 2.5 × 27 ÷ 2 = 33.75, or 34 bags. Comparing those two options also requires local bag and delivery prices; bag count alone does not establish which is cheaper.

Choose a depth that suits the bed

University of Minnesota Extension describes a 2- to 4-inch mulch layer for many garden uses and advises a clear ring around trunks and stems. University of Maryland Extension advises a maximum of 3 inches in its landscape-management guidance. These are general horticultural references, not a direction to add the same amount every season. Material type, plant type, drainage and existing depth matter. Check the layer in several spots before deciding how much new material to spread. See the University of Minnesota Extension mulch guidance and University of Maryland Extension landscape guidance for plant-care context.

Keep mulch off tree trunks and plant stems; do not build a mound around the base. A thick existing layer should be loosened or reduced where needed rather than buried under another full-depth delivery. Where mulch is used around a tree, measure the surface actually covered by the ring and exclude the open space at the trunk. The quantity calculation tells you volume; it cannot diagnose a plant's health or replace local horticultural advice.

New installation versus a top-up

A bare bed and an existing bed have different ordering needs. If a 500-square-foot bed has about 1.25 inches of mulch remaining and the intended total is 3 inches, the additional depth is 3 − 1.25 = 1.75 inches. The top-up volume is 500 × 1.75 ÷ 324 = 2.70 cubic yards before any allowance. Ordering for a full new 3-inch layer would be 4.63 cubic yards, nearly 1.93 yards more. Existing coverage is rarely perfectly even, so take several depth readings and use an honest range.

For beds with mixed bare patches and healthy coverage, split the area into sections. If one section needs 3 inches and another only 1 inch, calculate each section separately and add the volumes. Avoid using an average depth that hides a heavily depleted area. A Cubic Yard Calculator can help with individual rectangular sections, though its optional waste setting should reflect your own allowance rather than being added twice.

Low, working and high quantity scenarios

ScenarioMeasurement assumptionHow to use it
LowOnly visibly thin areas need a top-upMeasure those patches separately and keep sound existing material in place.
WorkingMeasured bed area needs the chosen additional depthUse area × inches ÷ 324 and round to the supplier's actual selling increment.
HighIrregular edges, settled spots or small measuring errors expand the volumeTest a transparent allowance and document why it is needed before ordering.

These are scope scenarios, not national price ranges. For the 280-square-foot example at 2.5 inches, the calculated 2.16 yards is the working volume. A 10% allowance produces about 2.38 yards, and rounding to the next half yard produces a possible 2.5-yard purchase. Do not treat the allowance as a substitute for measuring, and do not add it again if a landscaper's quote already includes a waste or overage factor.

What the quantity does and does not include

The calculated yards cover the mulch layer over the measured beds. They do not include removing weeds or old mulch, edging, landscape fabric, soil or compost amendments, grading, hauling, delivery, spreading labor, taxes or disposal. A contractor's installed quote may include some of these items. Ask for the product type, cubic yards, delivered quantity, bed-preparation tasks, spreading depth and cleanup in writing so bids can be compared on the same scope.

For a price estimate, multiply the actual purchase quantity by a supplier's current price per cubic yard, then add delivery and installation only if they are separate charges. Our mulch cost-per-yard guide shows why a low material rate can still result in a higher delivered bill. Do not invent a national price and apply it to every location: material availability, truck access, minimum orders and labor conditions differ by region.

Delivery, access and local rules

Before placing a bulk order, confirm where the truck can unload. A driveway drop may be straightforward; a narrow side gate, stairs or beds far from the curb can add carrying labor. Protect pavement and drains as appropriate and ask whether the supplier can place the pile where you need it. A simple mulch refresh usually does not require a building permit, but work in a public right-of-way, near protected trees or under HOA landscape rules may require local permission. Check your city or HOA requirements when those conditions apply.

For larger projects, ask whether the delivered “yard” is a measured loose volume and whether the supplier can make a second small delivery if you run short. A small underorder may cost another delivery fee; a large overorder leaves a pile that must be stored or disposed of. Record measurements, selected depth and supplier increments so the order can be adjusted rationally rather than guessed.

Common measurement mistakes

  • Using the entire yard area when only planting beds receive mulch.
  • Mixing inches of depth with feet of width without converting units.
  • Adding a full fresh layer on top of an already thick existing layer.
  • Counting paths, boulders, patios and open trunk rings as covered area.
  • Comparing bag prices and bulk prices without converting both to cubic feet or cubic yards.
  • Assuming a quote for “mulch” includes delivery, bed preparation and spreading.

For other landscape materials, the underlying volume conversion remains the same; the appropriate depth and placement rules may change. The cubic-yard measurement guide explains the unit conversion for soil, gravel and similar projects. Measure the actual surface and choose the material specification before ordering.

Planning takeaway: Calculate the uncovered bed area and the additional depth needed, convert with area × inches ÷ 324, then round according to the supplier's sales increments. Keep the intended total layer suitable for the plants and document delivery and labor separately.

Frequently asked questions

How many cubic yards cover 500 square feet at 3 inches?

500 × 3 ÷ 324 = 4.63 cubic yards before any allowance. If a supplier sells half-yard increments, ask whether 4.5 yards is enough for your measured conditions or whether to buy 5 yards.

How many square feet does one cubic yard of mulch cover?

At 3 inches, one cubic yard covers 324 ÷ 3 = 108 square feet. At 2 inches it covers 162 square feet. Actual spreading and uneven beds can change the purchased quantity.

Should I add mulch every year?

Measure the remaining layer first. If it is still at the desired depth, adding another full layer can be excessive. Replenish only what has thinned, and keep mulch away from trunks and stems.

Is bagged mulch or bulk mulch cheaper?

Convert both offers to the same volume and compare total material, delivery and handling costs. Bagged mulch may suit small or hard-to-access beds; bulk loads may suit larger areas, but local prices decide.

Do I need a permit to put mulch in my yard?

Ordinary mulch spreading generally is not a building-permit project. Public right-of-way work, protected trees or HOA rules can impose separate local requirements, so check before changing those areas.
