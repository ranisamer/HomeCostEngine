Furnace Replacement Cost: What to Budget in 2026

Understand furnace replacement cost in 2026, including equipment size, efficiency, fuel, venting, electrical work, ductwork and installation scope.

Published September 11, 2026 • Reviewed October 13, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

Quick answer: a furnace replacement budget should include the selected furnace, removal, sheet-metal transitions, filter arrangement, venting, condensate handling, electrical and control work, permits, startup and any necessary duct correction. Do not compare bids by the equipment price alone. First confirm the heating load and existing-system condition, then normalize every proposal to the same written scope.

Use the HVAC replacement cost calculator for an early scenario, not as a contractor quote or sizing tool. Replace its broad assumptions with local proposals that name the exact model, capacity, AFUE, included labor and exclusions.

A practical furnace replacement budget formula

Normalized project total = quoted base scope + known exclusions + selected allowances + required corrections.

Keep a known vent or electrical change in the project subtotal. Keep a contingency separate for conditions that cannot be confirmed until removal. This prevents a low base bid with several exclusions from appearing cheaper than a more complete proposal. It also avoids counting the same work once as an exclusion and again as contingency.

Low, typical and higher-complexity planning logic

Planning caseWhat the scope may look likeWhat to verify
Lower complexitySame fuel, compatible venting, sound ducts, accessible equipment and limited transition workWhether permit, disposal, thermostat and startup are included
Typical replacementNew transition pieces, filter cabinet, controls, condensate or modest vent changesExact model numbers, airflow setup and written allowances
Higher complexityFuel or efficiency change, chimney work, electrical upgrade, difficult access, duct correction or code-driven workWhich trade owns each item and what remains excluded

These categories describe scope, not national prices. Labor, fuel infrastructure, climate, access, permit rules and equipment availability vary by region. Obtain comparable local bids and confirm the applicable requirements with the local authority having jurisdiction.

Worked example: normalize two furnace quotes

Assume Proposal A is an illustrative $7,900 base bid. It excludes a $250 permit, a $600 return-air transition and a $1,200 chimney-liner allowance. Its known normalized total is $9,950 ($7,900 + $250 + $600 + $1,200). Proposal B is an illustrative $9,400 bid that includes the permit, return transition, new venting, condensate routing, thermostat, removal and startup.

Proposal B is $550 below Proposal A after the written scopes are aligned, even though its headline price is $1,500 higher. This is hypothetical quote arithmetic, not a market-price estimate. Before choosing, confirm whether the liner allowance in Proposal A is actually required, whether Proposal B's vent route is approved, and whether both bids specify equivalent equipment, warranty and airflow work. Enter the line items in the contractor quote comparison tool so included work and allowances remain visible.

Do not automatically replace like-for-like capacity

The old input capacity may reflect a rule of thumb, an earlier envelope or equipment that was oversized from the start. Ask for the design heating load and the assumptions used. ACCA Manual J is an ANSI-recognized residential load-calculation standard; square footage alone does not account for climate, insulation, windows, air leakage, orientation or duct location.

Ask the contractor to distinguish furnace input from output capacity and explain how the selected unit covers the design load. Larger is not automatically safer. Oversizing can affect cycling, comfort and distribution, while undersizing may not maintain the intended indoor condition at design weather.

Fuel, venting and condensate can change the project

Scope areaQuestions for the proposal
FuelIs the existing gas, propane, oil or electrical supply retained and verified?
VentingWill the furnace reuse a chimney, need a liner, or use a new approved intake/exhaust route?
CondensateWhere will condensate drain, and are a pump, neutralizer or freeze protection required?
Combustion airHow are combustion-air and clearance requirements addressed?
Electrical and controlsAre disconnect, wiring, thermostat and accessory controls included?

A higher-efficiency furnace can require different venting and drainage from the existing unit. AFUE describes seasonal fuel-use efficiency under a test method; it does not describe duct loss, installation quality or the full household energy bill. Compare the complete installed package rather than treating the AFUE number as the only difference.

Airflow and duct problems do not disappear

If the home has noisy returns, weak rooms, a dirty coil, frequent limit trips or uneven temperatures, ask whether external static pressure, filter sizing and supply/return airflow are part of the commissioning plan. A new blower connected to a restrictive system can preserve the same comfort problem. Require a written description of any duct correction instead of an undefined “modify as needed” allowance.

What a useful proposal should name

  • Exact furnace manufacturer, model, input/output capacity and AFUE
  • Method and assumptions used to select capacity
  • Thermostat, controls and filter cabinet or media
  • Supply and return transitions plus any duct correction
  • Venting, combustion air and condensate work
  • Fuel, electrical and accessory connections
  • Permit, removal, disposal, startup and commissioning
  • Manufacturer registration, labor warranty and maintenance conditions
  • Every allowance, exclusion and change-order unit price

When to compare a heat pump or dual-fuel option

If major replacement work is already required, request a separate alternative where a heat pump or dual-fuel system is practical. Compare electrical capacity, climate performance, backup heat, retained-furnace condition and duct requirements. The heat pump versus central air cost guide shows how to compare the complete installed scopes rather than one equipment price.

Contingency, rebates and regional caveats

Separate confirmed work from uncertainty. A documented chimney correction belongs in the subtotal; concealed damage discovered only after removal may belong in a reserve. Use the project contingency calculator to record the reason for a reserve instead of applying one universal percentage.

Do not count a rebate, tax credit or utility incentive until eligibility is confirmed for the exact equipment, installation date, residence and program rules. Requirements and funding can change. Save the model certificate, paid invoice, permit and commissioning records. Local codes may also affect vent termination, combustion air, condensate disposal, clearances and carbon-monoxide alarms.

Homeowner quote checklist

  1. Ask how the heating load and replacement capacity were determined.
  2. Record exact model numbers, output capacity and AFUE.
  3. Photograph the existing furnace, vent, filter, transitions and access path.
  4. Mark every included and excluded trade on each proposal.
  5. Normalize known exclusions before comparing totals.
  6. Keep contingency separate from work already known.
  7. Confirm permit responsibility, startup measurements and warranty terms.
  8. Save the final scope and assumptions in the free home project cost planning guide.

Frequently asked questions

Should I replace a furnace with the same size?

Not automatically. Ask for a current heating-load calculation and distinguish input from output capacity. The old unit may have been oversized or the home may have changed.

What is often excluded from a furnace quote?

Common exclusions can include duct correction, electrical upgrades, chimney or vent work, permits, thermostat, condensate drainage, hazardous-material work and repairs discovered after removal. Read the specific proposal.

Does a higher AFUE guarantee lower bills?

No. AFUE addresses fuel-use efficiency for the furnace. Weather, thermostat settings, ducts, envelope performance, fuel prices, sizing and installation also affect household cost.

Can a heat pump replace a furnace?

Sometimes. Climate, electrical capacity, the home's load, duct condition, backup strategy and local economics all matter. Compare a complete heat-pump or dual-fuel scope with the furnace proposal.

How many furnace quotes should I compare?

There is no universal number, but more than one complete written proposal helps reveal scope differences. Compare equivalent equipment, inclusions, allowances and warranties rather than headline totals alone.
