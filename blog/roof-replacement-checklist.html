Roof Replacement Checklist for Homeowners

Use one scope-first checklist from inspection through final payment so bids are comparable and important details do not disappear into assumptions.

Reviewed September 20, 2026 • HomeCostEngine Editorial Team

A roof replacement is easier to compare when every contractor responds to the same written scope. A low proposal may exclude flashing, permits, deck repairs or disposal that another proposal includes. This checklist helps homeowners define the work, document uncertainties and verify completion without pretending that an online guide can inspect a roof.

1. Record the reason for replacement

  • Document active leaks, storm damage, missing covering, granule loss or recurring repair areas.
  • Photograph interior stains and accessible attic conditions without climbing onto the roof.
  • Separate urgent water-entry work from elective appearance or efficiency upgrades.
  • If insurance may be involved, preserve damage evidence and follow the insurer's inspection process before permanent work.

A qualified inspection should determine whether repair or full replacement is appropriate. Age alone does not prove failure, and a leak does not always require replacing the entire covering. Use the condition-first guide to how long a roof lasts when the installation date is known but the remaining service life is uncertain.

2. Establish roof quantity and a planning range

Living area is not roof area. Measure or obtain the horizontal roof plan, include relevant overhangs, and adjust for pitch. Divide surface area by 100 to obtain roofing squares. The Roofing Square Calculator helps organize the quantity before bids arrive.

Worked example: a measured horizontal roof plan is 2,200 sq ft and the roof has a 6:12 pitch factor of about 1.118. Surface area is 2,200 × 1.118 = 2,460 sq ft, or 24.6 squares. A hypothetical 10% material-planning allowance produces 27.1 squares. The contractor must confirm the actual takeoff and product-specific waste.

3. Compare low, typical and high scope—not generic prices

  • Low scope: simple shape, straightforward access, one tear-off layer, sound deck, few penetrations and standard local requirements.
  • Typical scope: full tear-off and disposal, ordinary valleys and penetrations, limited deck allowance, flashing replacement, ventilation review and permit.
  • High scope: steep or complex geometry, difficult staging, multiple layers, extensive sheathing repair, chimney or skylight work, specialty covering or resilience upgrades.

Enter the confirmed area and local scope assumptions in the Roof Replacement Cost Calculator. These tiers organize uncertainty; they are not national price claims.

4. Vet each contractor

  • Verify the business identity, physical contact information and recent references.
  • Check licensing or registration with the appropriate state or local agency where required.
  • Request current general liability and workers' compensation evidence applicable to the work.
  • Ask who supervises the site and who may perform subcontracted work.
  • Confirm experience with the exact material system and roof geometry.

Do not rely only on a badge, review count or manufacturer label. Verify credentials with the issuing organization and read what the designation actually covers.

5. Require an itemized scope

The proposal should identify tear-off layers, disposal, deck inspection, underlayment, leak barrier where specified, covering product, starter, ridge or hip cap, drip edge, flashing, pipe boots, fasteners, ventilation, site protection, cleanup and permit responsibility. It should also list exclusions such as gutters, fascia, soffit, skylights, chimney repairs and interior water damage.

Use the Roofing Quote Comparison Guide to align proposals before comparing totals.

6. Define hidden deck repairs in advance

Existing covering can conceal rotten or delaminated sheathing. The contract should state whether any deck replacement is included, how additional work is measured, the unit-price or approval method, and what photo documentation will be provided. Written authorization protects both parties when concealed conditions appear.

7. Confirm the complete material system

Record manufacturer, product line, color, exposure and accessory components. Verify underlayment, eave or valley protection, starter, field covering, cap, flashing and ventilation compatibility. Ask which instructions control installation and who registers any manufacturer warranty.

In high-wind or hail regions, discuss enhanced attachment, sealed roof deck, stronger edges or impact-resistant products. The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety describes coordinated severe-weather measures in its FORTIFIED Roof guidance. That voluntary standard may exceed local code.

8. Review flashing and penetrations

  • Identify valleys, walls, chimneys, skylights, plumbing vents and mechanical curbs.
  • State whether existing flashing will be replaced, reused or evaluated after tear-off.
  • Document how siding, masonry or stucco interfaces will be handled.
  • Confirm satellite dishes, solar mounts and abandoned penetrations.

“Replace roof” is not specific enough to define these transitions, which are common water-entry points.

9. Evaluate ventilation and moisture

Confirm intake and exhaust as a system rather than adding vents by habit. Bathroom or dryer exhaust should not terminate into the attic. Ask whether signs of condensation, blocked intake, wet insulation or mold require separate evaluation. Roof covering cannot correct every attic moisture problem.

10. Verify permits and local requirements

Contact the city or county building department to confirm permits, inspections, adopted code and rules for tear-off or re-covering. Wildfire zones, coastal wind regions, historic districts and homeowners associations may add requirements. The contract should identify who obtains the permit and closes inspections.

11. Put schedule, weather and logistics in writing

  • Approximate start window and expected working duration.
  • Material delivery, dumpster and equipment locations.
  • Protection for landscaping, siding, windows, driveways and attic contents.
  • Daily dry-in and weather-delay plan.
  • Access, pets, gates, vehicles, noise and working-hour expectations.
  • Nail collection and final debris removal.

12. Treat safety as required scope

Roofing exposes workers and occupants to falls, falling objects and material-handling hazards. Keep family members away from work zones and do not climb onto the roof to monitor progress. The federal OSHA residential fall-protection resources explain employer responsibilities for covered work. A contractor's safety plan should not be removed merely to lower a bid.

13. Review contract and payment terms

The signed agreement should match the final proposal and include parties, property, scope, products, price structure, payment milestones, change-order process, estimated schedule, cleanup, permit responsibility and warranties. Check state rules governing deposits, cancellation rights, notices and lien waivers. Avoid paying for undocumented work or approving important changes verbally.

Place normalized proposals in the Contractor Quote Comparison tool so missing scope remains visible.

14. Separate workmanship and product warranties

Manufacturer coverage and contractor workmanship coverage are different. Record term, covered components, exclusions, transfer rules, registration deadlines, claim contact and whether maintenance or ventilation conditions affect coverage. Keep contracts, invoices, photos, permits and product identifiers together.

15. Final walkthrough before payment

  • Review completed covering, edges, flashing, penetrations, vents and gutters from safe viewpoints.
  • Confirm approved change orders and documented deck replacement.
  • Check yard, driveway, attic and landscaping for debris or damage.
  • Receive permit sign-off where required, warranty documents and paid invoice.
  • Photograph completed work and save maintenance instructions.
Planning sequence: confirm roof quantity, normalize scope, verify credentials, sign a detailed contract, document changes and complete a final walkthrough before the last payment.

Frequently asked questions

How many roofing bids should I collect?

Collect enough qualified proposals to compare scope and communication, often at least two or three when timing allows. More bids do not help if each describes different work.

Should the lowest bid win?

Not automatically. Normalize materials, tear-off, flashing, ventilation, permits, deck allowances, cleanup and warranty before comparing totals.

What if damaged decking is found?

Use the contract's documented unit-price or change-order process. Request photos and measured quantities before authorizing additional work when conditions permit.

Who should obtain the roofing permit?

Local rules vary. Confirm with the building department and state clearly in the contract who applies, pays, schedules inspections and closes the permit.

When should final payment be made?

Follow the contract and applicable law, typically after agreed work, cleanup, documentation and required inspections are complete—not merely when the crew leaves.
